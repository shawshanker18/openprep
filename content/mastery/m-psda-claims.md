## What this skill is

A study supports only the claims its design allows. At mastery level you judge how far a result can be generalised, whether a cause can be claimed, and what a confounding variable might explain.

> **Judge the design first.** Ask who was studied, how they were chosen, and whether anything was assigned.

## Learn the idea

**Try this first.** A random sample of $500$ voters in one city finds $55\%$ support a policy. Can we say $55\%$ of the country supports it?

**Let's reason it through.**

1. Ask who was studied: a random sample of voters in one city. Why does this matter? Random selection lets results generalise, but only to the population the sample came from.
2. The population is the city's voters, not the whole country. So the result describes the city.
3. To claim the country, you would need a sample of the country, or a reason to believe the city matches it.
4. The claim should be limited to the city's voters.

**Your turn.** Students are randomly assigned to two teaching methods. Method B scores higher. What can the study claim?

**Answer:** Random assignment supports a cause-and-effect claim: Method B likely raises scores for these students. Because they were not randomly sampled from a larger population, the claim is limited to these students.

## Formulas and rules

**Key formulas and rules**

| Rule | What it says |
|---|---|
| Random selection | Results generalise to the population sampled, and no further. |
| Random assignment | Differences between groups can be attributed to the treatment. |
| No random assignment | The result is only an association. |
| Confounding | A third variable can explain an association. |

**Desmos check, step by step:**

1. Desmos cannot judge a study design, so use the checklist instead.
2. Ask: was the sample random? Was a treatment assigned? If not, the strongest claim is an association.

## Worked example 1 (easy)

*Students who eat breakfast get better grades. Can this study show breakfast causes better grades?*

1. Breakfast was not assigned, so the result is an association.
2. A confounding variable, such as sleep or household income, could explain both breakfast and grades.

## Worked example 2 (SAT-level)

*A school randomly selects $50$ students and randomly assigns half to extra tutoring. The tutored group scores higher. What can be claimed about tutoring, and about the school?*

1. Random assignment supports a cause-and-effect claim for these $50$ students.
2. Because the $50$ were randomly selected from the school, the result can be generalised to the school's students.
3. The claim should not go beyond the school without more evidence.

## Common traps

- **Claiming cause from an observational study.**
- **Generalising beyond the sampled population.**
- **Forgetting confounding.** Ask what else could explain the result.

## The fast way (and when to use Desmos)

Desmos does not judge studies, so use the two-question checklist: who was studied, and was anything assigned. Then write the strongest claim the answers support.
