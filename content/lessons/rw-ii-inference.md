## What this skill is

Inference questions give you a short text that ends with a blank and ask, "Which choice most logically completes the text?" The text lays out facts, a study, or an argument, and you have to supply the conclusion those facts lead to. The topics range across science, social studies, and the humanities, but no outside knowledge is needed.

## Learn the idea

**Try this first.** "Every sample from Lake X contained the algae, and no sample from Lake Y did." What can you infer about Lake X? Separate what the text reports from what it implies.

**Let's work it out.**

1. Start with what the text reports. Every Lake X sample had algae. Why is that the starting point? An inference must build on the facts the text gives, not on outside knowledge.
2. Take one reasoning step. If every sample has algae, the lake likely contains algae. The step is small and direct.
3. Watch the strength of the word. "Likely" is the right size for this step. "Proves" would go too far, because samples are a small look at a lake.
4. Check what is not said. The text does not say how much algae there is, so any answer about quantity is a leap.

**Your turn.** "Every student who studied two hours passed the test. Maria studied two hours." What can be inferred?

**Answer:** Maria likely passed. The text supports this by stating that every two-hour student passed. It does not guarantee it, so "likely" fits better than "certainly."

## Formulas and rules

**Key rules**

| Rule | What it says |
|---|---|
| One step | An inference is the next step the text clearly supports. |
| Modest words | "Likely" and "suggests" fit; "proves" and "always" rarely do. |
| Limits from design | What a study did not test limits what it can conclude. |

| Wrong-answer type | Why it fails |
|---|---|
| Overreach | Goes beyond the evidence ("all," "always," "proves") |
| Reversal | Flips the cause and effect, or the comparison |
| Unrelated true-sounding fact | Plausible in real life, but not connected to the text |
| Answers a different question | Draws a conclusion about something the text mentions only in passing |

Rule: **predict the ending before you read the choices.** Cover the choices, finish the sentence in your own words, then match.

## Worked example 1 (easy)

> In a survey of a town's community center, staff found that attendance at evening classes had dropped sharply since the town's only bus route stopped running after 6 p.m. Attendance at morning and afternoon classes did not change. These results suggest that ______

A) residents have lost interest in the center's classes.
B) the lack of evening bus service may be keeping some residents from attending evening classes.
C) the center should cancel all of its evening classes.
D) morning classes are more popular than afternoon classes.

Prediction: "the bus change is hurting evening attendance."

1. A is contradicted: daytime attendance is steady, so interest has not dropped.
2. C is a recommendation, not a conclusion the data supports.
3. D compares morning and afternoon, which the text never does.
4. **B** links the only change (bus times) to the only affected classes (evening). Answer: B.

## Worked example 2 (SAT-level)

> Historians studying a medieval monastery's library found that dozens of its manuscripts contain notes in the margins written in a local dialect rather than in Latin, the language of the main text. Because monks at the monastery were trained to write only in Latin, some historians have argued that the notes were added by visitors. However, the monastery's records show it rarely admitted visitors to its library, and several notes refer to daily tasks at the monastery. Taken together, these details suggest that ______

A) the manuscripts were originally written in the local dialect.
B) visitors to the monastery wrote most of the notes.
C) some people who lived and worked at the monastery may have written in the dialect despite their training.
D) the monks did not understand Latin well enough to read the manuscripts.

1. The text sets up a claim (visitors wrote the notes) and then gives two problems with it: few visitors, and notes about monastery chores.
2. B repeats the claim the text is undercutting.
3. A contradicts the text: the main text is Latin.
4. D overreaches; nothing suggests the monks could not read Latin.
5. **C** explains both details and stays modest ("may"). Answer: C.

## Common traps

- **Choosing the claim the passage is arguing against.** When you see "some argue... however," the answer usually goes with the "however."
- **Overreach.** "The study proves" or "all species" from one small study.
- **Using one detail and ignoring another.** The correct answer must account for every clue the text gives.
- **Outside knowledge.** A fact you know from class is not an inference from this text.
- **Recommendations instead of conclusions.** "The city should..." is usually wrong unless the text is about what to do.

## The fast way (and when to use Desmos)

Desmos is not used for Reading and Writing questions. The fastest approach is:

1. Read the text and identify the claim, the evidence, and any "however."
2. Predict the ending in a few words before looking at the choices.
3. Eliminate overreach and reversals first; they are the easiest to spot.
4. Between two survivors, pick the one that accounts for **every** clue in the text.

These are among the harder R&W questions. If you are stuck after about 90 seconds, eliminate what you can, guess, flag it, and come back.
