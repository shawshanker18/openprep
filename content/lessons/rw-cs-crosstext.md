## What this skill is

Cross-Text Connections questions give you two short texts on the same topic, labeled **Text 1** and **Text 2**. You are asked how the author of one would respond to the other ("Based on the texts, how would the author of Text 2 most likely respond to...?"), or what both authors would agree on. You are comparing *viewpoints*, not just facts.

## Key ideas

- Usually, Text 1 presents a claim, finding, or recommendation, and Text 2 reacts to it.
- Text 2's reaction is rarely total rejection. Most often it **qualifies** the claim: "true, but incomplete," "true here, but not everywhere," or "another factor matters too."
- Answer from **Text 2's** point of view when asked how Text 2 would respond. Use only what Text 2 says or clearly implies.
- For "both would agree" questions, the answer must fit **both** texts. Anything only one text supports is wrong.
- Pin down exactly *what* in Text 1 is being responded to: the evidence, the conclusion, or the recommendation.

## Formulas and rules

| Text 2's relationship to Text 1 | Typical wording in Text 2 | Typical correct answer |
|---|---|---|
| Qualifies | "but this overlooks," "only part of the story" | "the claim is incomplete" / "overstates" |
| Limits scope | "in other settings," "for some groups" | "may not apply to all cases" |
| Offers another cause | "another factor," "may instead be due to" | "something else may also explain it" |
| Agrees and extends | "further supports," "builds on" | "would agree, and add..." |
| Rejects | "contradicts," "no evidence" (rare) | "would disagree because..." |

Rule: **summarize each text in one sentence, then write one sentence connecting them** before reading choices.

## Worked example 1 (easy)

> **Text 1**
> City officials argue that planting street trees mainly benefits neighborhoods by making them more attractive to visitors.
>
> **Text 2**
> A team of urban researchers measured summer temperatures on streets with and without trees. Tree-lined streets were up to 4 degrees cooler, reducing residents' use of air conditioning.

*Based on the texts, how would the researchers in Text 2 most likely respond to the officials' argument in Text 1?*

A) By agreeing that attractiveness is the only benefit of street trees
B) By suggesting that the argument overlooks another important benefit of street trees
C) By arguing that street trees make neighborhoods less attractive
D) By claiming that visitors prefer streets without trees

Connection: "Text 2 shows a cooling benefit Text 1 ignores."

1. A contradicts Text 2, which shows a second benefit.
2. C and D are not supported; Text 2 says nothing about attractiveness or visitors.
3. **B**: Text 2 adds a benefit without denying Text 1's point. Answer: B.

## Worked example 2 (SAT-level)

> **Text 1**
> In a six-month trial, employees at a software company who switched to a four-day workweek completed as many projects as before and reported less stress. Some economists conclude that a four-day week would benefit workplaces generally.
>
> **Text 2**
> Trials of shorter workweeks have mostly involved office jobs in which output depends on focused individual work. In settings such as hospitals or factories, where staff must cover fixed shifts, reducing hours can create staffing gaps that cancel out any gains.

*Based on the texts, how would the author of Text 2 most likely respond to the economists' conclusion in Text 1?*

A) By rejecting it, since the trial's results were probably exaggerated
B) By cautioning that the conclusion may not apply to every type of workplace
C) By agreeing that hospitals and factories should adopt a four-day week
D) By arguing that office workers experience more stress than factory workers

1. Text 1: the trial succeeded, so economists generalize to "workplaces generally."
2. Text 2: shift-based workplaces are different.
3. A: Text 2 never questions the trial's results.
4. C: the opposite of Text 2's concern.
5. D: stress levels across jobs are never compared.
6. **B** captures Text 2's *limit on scope*. Answer: B.

## Common traps

- **Too extreme.** "Reject entirely" when Text 2 only qualifies. Match the strength of Text 2's language.
- **Answering from Text 1's perspective.** Re-read who is responding to whom.
- **Agreeing on the topic, not the view.** Both texts discussing gardens doesn't mean both hold the same opinion about them.
- **Responding to the wrong part.** Text 2 may accept Text 1's data but reject its conclusion. The answer must target the conclusion.
- **Using details only one text mentions** in a "both would agree" question.

## The fast way (and when to use Desmos)

Desmos is not used for Reading and Writing questions. The fastest approach is:

1. Read the question first so you know which text is responding.
2. Read Text 1 and find its main claim in a few words.
3. Read Text 2 and decide: agree, qualify, limit, add a cause, or reject?
4. Eliminate choices whose strength doesn't match (most often, "rejects" when Text 2 only qualifies).

Budget about 90 seconds; two texts take longer to read.
