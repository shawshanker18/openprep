## What this skill is

Central Ideas and Details questions ask what a short text is mostly about, or what it says about one specific point. You will see two kinds of question: **main idea** ("Which choice best states the main idea of the text?") and **detail** ("According to the text, what does Marisol do...?" or "Based on the text, how do X differ from Y?"). Passages can be informational (science, history, the arts) or literary (a short excerpt from a story).

## Learn the idea

**Try this first.** "Birds migrate for food. Yet researchers now think many migrate mainly to find safer places to breed." What is the main idea of this passage?

**Let's work it out.**

1. Find the turn. The word "Yet" signals that the author is about to shift away from the first idea. The main idea usually comes after such a turn.
2. Compare the two claims. The first is the common view, that birds migrate for food. The second is what researchers now think, and the author presents it as the real point.
3. Ask whether every sentence fits the second claim. The passage is short, and its focus is the newer explanation, so the main idea is the breeding claim.
4. Why not choose the first claim? A main idea must be the point the text emphasises. The first claim is what the author moves away from.

**Your turn.** "Many assume that museums mainly store objects. In fact, their most important work is education, and storage is only a means to it." What is the main idea?

**Answer:** Museums' most important work is education. "In fact" turns from the assumption to the real point, and storage is described as a means to that purpose.

## Formulas and rules

**Key rules**

| Rule | What it says |
|---|---|
| The whole text | The main idea must be supported by every sentence. |
| Turns signal the point | "However," "but," and "in fact" often introduce the real point. |
| Details are stated | A detail answer must be directly stated or a close paraphrase. |

| Wrong-answer type | What it looks like | Test |
|---|---|---|
| Too narrow | True detail, but only one sentence of the text | Does it cover the *whole* text? |
| Too broad | Claims about "all," "most," or a bigger topic | Does the text actually go that far? |
| Distorted | Uses the text's words but changes the relationship | Does the cause, comparison, or opinion match? |
| Off-topic | Reasonable but never mentioned | Can you point to the line? |

Rule: **summarize the text in your own words in about ten words before reading the choices**. Then pick the choice closest to your summary.

### Main idea versus main topic

The main topic is what the text is about (a painter, a plant, a play). The main idea is what the author says about that topic. Choices that name only the topic are usually too narrow or too vague.

### What the text suggests

"Most strongly suggests" asks for a conclusion the text supports even though it is not stated. Find the evidence the text gives, then choose the choice that the evidence most directly supports, without going beyond it.

### Tone and word choice

What a narrator says about a place or a person is shown by the words chosen: vivid detail, loaded adjectives, or a calm, factual tone. For a question about the narrator's characterization, choose the answer that matches those words, not the plot.

## Worked example 1 (easy)

> Many people assume that ancient bridges were built by trial and error. However, records from one Roman province show that engineers tested small model arches before building full-sized ones. They recorded which shapes held the most weight and used those results to plan their bridges.

*Which choice best states the main idea of the text?*

A) Roman bridges were stronger than modern bridges.
B) Some ancient engineers used planned testing rather than guesswork.
C) Roman engineers built model arches from stone.
D) Most ancient bridges collapsed within a century.

Your summary: "Romans tested models first, not trial and error."

1. A is off-topic: modern bridges are never mentioned.
2. C is too narrow, and "from stone" is not stated.
3. D is off-topic.
4. **B** matches the contrast set up by "However." Answer: B.

## Worked example 2 (SAT-level)

> The following text is from a story. Teo has worked in his uncle's bakery every summer.
>
> Teo had always complained about the early mornings, the flour in his hair, the endless trays. But on his last day before leaving for college, he arrived an hour early. He kneaded the dough slowly, as if memorizing the feel of it, and when his uncle offered to let him leave at noon, Teo shook his head and reached for another tray.

*Which choice best states the main idea of the text?*

A) Teo is relieved that he will no longer have to work early mornings.
B) Teo's uncle wants Teo to stay and run the bakery.
C) Despite past complaints, Teo is reluctant to leave the bakery behind.
D) Teo has become a more skilled baker than his uncle.

1. A contradicts the text: he arrives early and refuses to leave.
2. B is about the uncle, and the uncle actually offers to let him go.
3. D is never stated; no comparison of skill is made.
4. **C** covers both halves: the old complaints ("had always complained") and the new behavior ("memorizing," "shook his head"). Answer: C.

## Common traps

- **Picking a true detail as the main idea.** "Teo kneaded the dough slowly" is true, but it is evidence for the main idea, not the main idea itself.
- **Stopping at the first sentence.** Many texts open with a view that the author then corrects. The correction is the point.
- **Bringing in outside knowledge.** If the text does not say it, it is not the answer, even if it is true in real life.
- **Extreme words.** "Always," "never," "only," and "proves" are red flags unless the text uses equally strong language.
- **Ignoring the intro line** on literary passages. It often tells you who the character is and what situation they are in.

## The fast way (and when to use Desmos)

Desmos is not used for Reading and Writing questions. The fastest approach is:

1. Read the question first so you know whether it is main idea or detail.
2. Read the text once, noticing any turn word ("however," "but," "yet").
3. For main idea, say a ten-word summary in your head. For detail, find the exact line.
4. Eliminate choices that are too narrow, too broad, or unsupported. Between two survivors, pick the one you can prove line by line.

Aim for about 60 to 75 seconds per question.
